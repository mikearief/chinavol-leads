Soybeans and Rare Earths: The Summit Headfake Hiding China's Slowdown

May 20, 2026 · ChinaVol Market Intelligence

Context

The Trump-Xi summit in Beijing produced the headlines Washington and Beijing both wanted: a $17 billion soybean purchase pledge, rare-earth cooperation, and a preliminary agreement to reduce some tariffs. Markets reflexively cheered. The Hang Seng Index (^HSI) had rallied into the meeting, and U.S.-listed China ADRs saw a modest relief bid. But beneath the diplomacy, the data is screaming the opposite of a thaw. China's economy lost steam in April, with retail sales hitting a 40-month low. Beijing is simultaneously trimming U.S. Treasury holdings to an 18-year low. And within 48 hours of Trump's departure, Vladimir Putin landed in Beijing to test the "strategic triangle." The summit was theater. The structural decoupling is accelerating.

What Happened

On May 14–15, Presidents Trump and Xi met in Beijing. The White House touted deals on soybeans and rare earths, while China's Ministry of Commerce signaled preliminary tariff cuts and improved farm market access. According to Reuters, market watchers expect a 10% cut in soybean tariffs, which could allow private Chinese crushers to resume purchases. The New York Times reported that both sides struck a preliminary agreement to reduce some tariffs — language nearly identical to the Phase One playbook of 2020.

But the economic reality diverges sharply from the diplomatic narrative. CNBC reported that China's economy lost steam in April as retail sales hit a 40-month low, exposing persistent domestic demand weakness that no soybean deal can fix. At the same time, foreign governments are offloading U.S. Treasurys, with China trimming its holdings to an 18-year low. That is not the behavior of a country locking itself into a durable trade partnership with the United States. It is the behavior of a country diversifying away from dollar assets while keeping the diplomatic door open just wide enough to avoid escalation.

Then came Putin. Days after Trump left Beijing, the Russian president arrived for his own summit with Xi, as reported by CNBC under the headline "Putin heads to Beijing days after Trump in test of China's balancing act." Beijing is playing both sides of the great-power board. The "strategic triangle" — Washington, Moscow, Beijing — is under active stress, and China is positioning itself as the swing player. That does not suggest a stable, cooperative U.S.-China commercial framework. It suggests managed tension with tactical pauses.

In the equity market, the reaction has been mixed. Alibaba (BABA) is trading at $135.64, up modestly on the session but still down 7.95% year-to-date after an earnings quarter that revealed an 84% drop in adjusted EBITA. Pinduoduo (PDD) is at $97.34, up 2.5% and outperforming the complex thanks to its global Temu revenue and 22.66% profit margin. The KraneShares China Internet ETF (KWEB) is trading near $28.19, within striking distance of its 52-week low of $27.62. The iShares China Large-Cap ETF (FXI) is at $36.28, down 6.18% year-to-date and heavily weighted in financial services and consumer cyclicals — the exact sectors exposed to a domestic demand collapse.

Why It Matters

Investors are conflating diplomatic process with economic substance. A soybean purchase pledge and a 10% tariff trim on farm goods do not reverse decoupling. They manage it. The Phase One agreement collapsed under its own weight because enforcement was absent and China's structural subsidies were never addressed. The current framework looks like a shorter, weaker replay.

The Treasury dump is the more important signal. If Beijing is reducing dollar-asset exposure to an 18-year low while negotiating trade deals, the trade deals are tactical cover for a strategic exit. That exit has direct implications for U.S. interest rates, dollar strength, and the relative attractiveness of yuan-denominated assets. It also means that any "thaw" is likely to be narrow, reversible, and heavily conditional.

Domestically, the 40-month low in retail sales is a flashing red light. China's consumer engine is sputtering at a time when Beijing is trying to project stability. The property sector drag, youth unemployment concerns, and deflationary pressure in manufacturing are not resolved by buying American soybeans. They require fiscal stimulus and credit expansion that Beijing has been reluctant to deploy at scale. Without that stimulus, domestic-facing China equities will continue to underperform.

Second-Order Effects

The immediate second-order effect is a credibility gap between narrative and price action. China ADRs and Hong Kong-listed tech names rallied into the summit on hope. Now that the event is over and the outcome is hollow, those flows will reverse. Watch for KWEB to retest its 52-week low near $27.62 as foreign funds pull back.

Within the sector, the divergence between global-commerce names and domestic-facing names will widen. PDD's Temu business generates USD-denominated revenue from Western consumers and operates largely outside the U.S.-China diplomatic crosshairs. Its trailing P/E of roughly 10 and forward P/E below 8 make it a valuation anomaly in a sector where most names are deteriorating. By contrast, JD.com (JD), at $32.38, is tied to domestic logistics and consumption — both directly exposed to the retail-sales slowdown. JD's 1.05% profit margin and negative return on assets make it a structurally weak player in a weak macro.

The Treasury sell-off also has implications for global rates. If China continues to reduce its holdings, U.S. long-term yields could face upward pressure, tightening financial conditions and reducing the scope for Federal Reserve easing. That dynamic would hit growth equities globally, including U.S. tech, and further compress the multiples of China ADRs that trade as a high-beta proxy for global liquidity.

The Trade

The contrarian setup is to fade the summit optimism and position for the structural stress that the diplomacy is masking:

  • Short KWEB via June $27 puts or $28/$29 bear put spreads. The ETF is trading near $28.19, and a retest of the 52-week low is likely as post-summit flows reverse. The 7.42% headline yield is a trap if the underlying names keep bleeding.
  • Short FXI outright or via put spreads. At $36.28, the fund is heavily weighted in financial services (34.73%) and consumer cyclicals (27.32%) — the two sectors most exposed to domestic credit stress and weak retail demand. Its 9.97 P/E looks cheap until earnings revise lower.
  • Long PDD ($97.34) as a relative-value hedge. PDD's Temu revenue is global, its balance sheet is clean (total debt-to-equity of 1.30%), and its 26.93% return on equity is the best in the peer group. It is the only China internet name where the fundamentals are improving while the geopolitical exposure is shrinking.

For equity-only accounts, the pair trade is trim KWEB/FXI exposure and rotate into PDD. For options-enabled accounts, a 1×2 put spread on BABA ($135/$125 June) captures the post-earnings drift lower without paying peak volatility premium.

Risk Check

The primary risk is a genuine policy surprise. If Washington and Beijing negotiate a broad, enforceable tariff reduction on nonstrategic consumer goods — beyond the narrow farm carve-out — FXI and KWEB could snap back 5–7% in a short squeeze. That scenario is low-probability but high-impact, and it requires a "Board of Trade" enforcement mechanism that has not yet materialized.

BABA's AI and cloud capex could pay off in 2027 if its Qwen models capture meaningful enterprise share, reversing the margin implosion that crushed its latest quarter. The stock is already down 29% from its 52-week high, so some bad news is priced in — but not all of it.

PDD is not bulletproof. The EU is sharpening regulatory scrutiny on Temu's supply-chain compliance, and any action there would hit the one clean shirt in the drawer. Additionally, a global consumer slowdown would hurt Temu revenue growth even if geopolitical risk remains muted.

Finally, remember that China equities trade on narrative velocity, not valuation. A single tweet from Trump or an unexpected Xi policy speech can reprice the complex overnight. Size positions for volatility, not certainty. The summit headfake is already showing cracks — the question is how fast the market wakes up.
